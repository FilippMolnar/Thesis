\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chapter:introduction}

The living cell is in constant motion.
Proteins diffuse, assemble, and disassemble; organelles reshape and migrate;
signalling cascades propagate on timescales of seconds to minutes.
Capturing these dynamic processes demands simultaneous high spatial and temporal
resolution, a combination that conventional optical microscopy cannot provide.
The diffraction limit of light restricts spatial resolution to roughly
200--300\,nm~\cite{born1999principles}, far larger than the nanometre-scale
structures that govern cellular function. These structures are entirely hidden within the
diffraction blur.
Resolving these structures is important because their shape
and arrangement determine how a cell works. To truly understand and combat complex threats
like cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and infections, we must observe how they operate
inside living cells.

Fluorescence microscopy addresses this challenge by labelling structures of 
interest with fluorescent molecules (fluorophores). Upon excitation with a laser, 
fluorophores emit light that can be detected and imaged. 
Several super-resolution microscopy techniques have been developed to surpass
the diffraction limit and recover sub-diffraction information.
However, these methods typically involve significant trade-offs between
spatial resolution, acquisition speed, phototoxicity, hardware complexity,
and computational cost.
These trade-offs are especially acute in live cells, where high excitation
intensity and long exposures cause phototoxicity and photobleaching that damage
the studied sample. Live-cell imaging is therefore constrained to low
excitation power and short exposure times, which limit the number of photons
collected per frame. This
low photon budget translates directly into images with
low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).
Any practical method must be able to resolve the structures at this regime.

% Among these trade-offs, temporal resolution is often the first to be
% sacrificed, yet it is precisely what live-cell imaging cannot do without.
% Many of the most important cellular events are transient and unpredictable:
% vesicle fusion, protein recruitment to a damage site, or the brief
% reorganisation of the cytoskeleton can occur within seconds and never repeat in
% the same way. Methods that reconstruct an image offline from a long acquisition
% force the observer to commit to a fixed time window before knowing what will
% happen, so rare or fast events are easily missed or averaged away. Real-time
% super-resolution, by contrast, lets the experimenter watch dynamics as they
% unfold and intervene during live experiments---for example, following a moving
% organelle or triggering a perturbation at the right moment. This closes the
% loop between observation and control, turning microscopy from a record of what
% already happened into a tool for interacting with living systems.

Structured Illumination Microscopy (SIM)~\cite{sim2000,gustafsson2000sim} 
and Stimulated Emission Depletion (STED)~\cite{sted1994} 
achieve super-resolution through specialised optical hardware and 
illumination strategies.
Single-Molecule Localisation Microscopy (SMLM) methods \cite{betzig_2006_imaging,rust_2006_subdiffractionlimit}, 
achieve even higher resolution by localising individual fluorophores. 
However, SMLM requires sparse stochastic blinking and typically relies on 
tens of thousands of frames to reconstruct a single super-resolved image. 
Acquisition times can therefore range from minutes to hours, 
severely limiting temporal resolution and precluding real-time observation of 
dynamic cellular processes.

Fluctuation-based methods (Super-Resolution Optical Fluctuation Imaging
(SOFI)~\cite{sofi2009} and Super-Resolution Radial Fluctuations
(SRRF)~\cite{srrf2016}) offer a more favourable balance between spatial and temporal resolution.
These methods operate with dense fluorophore labelling and can achieve up to n-fold spatial improvement using higher order statistics.
 
Despite these advantages, SOFI still requires hundreds of frames per
reconstruction.
Each SR image is computed offline from the entire acquired sequence, with
computational latency in the range of seconds even with GPU
acceleration~\cite{resurf2024}.
This is incompatible with real-time live-cell imaging.

Deep learning offers a promising route to reduce both the number of frames
required and the inference latency.
Rather than computing cumulants analytically, a neural network can learn to map
a short sequence of low-resolution (LR) fluorescence frames directly to a
second-order SR image, effectively approximating the statistical operation from
far fewer observations.

Tekpinar et al.~\cite{resurf2024} introduced
SOFI-MISRGRU, adapting the Multi-Image Super-Resolution GRU
(MISRGRU) architecture~\cite{MISRGRU-original} (originally developed for
satellite imagery) to the fluorescence microscopy domain.
The model demonstrated a
400-fold latency reduction relative to conventional SOFI establishing the
practical feasibility of real-time deep-learning SR microscopy.

In this thesis we explore whether the transformer-based fusion module TR-MISR~\cite{TR-MISR-original}
can match or surpass the SR quality of the SOFI-MISRGRU~\cite{resurf2024} baseline on simulated
fluorescence microscopy data across varying SNR conditions.

Like MISRGRU, TR-MISR was originally developed for the PROBA-V challenge~\cite{TR-MISR-original},
a satellite multi-image super-resolution benchmark in which several low-resolution
Earth-observation images of the same scene, captured at different revisit times, are fused into
a single high-resolution image. 
On this benchmark TR-MISR outperformed MISRGRU and proved more
robust to noisy inputs, a property its authors attribute to the attention mechanism. Whether
this advantage transfers to the fluorescence-microscopy setting, where the input is a temporal
sequence of blinking frames rather than multiple satellite views, is an open question this thesis
addresses.

Secondly, we also extend SOFI-MISRGRU into a continuous-output streaming architecture 
that eliminates the batch acquisition bottleneck entirely. With this design the model
can combine more input images, which enhances the reconstruction quality while
enabling uninterrupted super-resolution video generation from dynamic samples in real-time.


\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

The SOFI-MISRGRU baseline establishes that deep learning can recover second-order SOFI
quality from a handful of frames, but it leaves two limitations open. Its ConvGRU fusion is
\emph{sequential}, so each frame must wait for the previous hidden state, and it aggregates
frames through an unnormalised summation whose magnitude grows with the number of inputs,
preventing the model from generalising beyond its training frame count. Secondly, the GAP summates 
redundant similar information together. Both limitations are
addressed in this thesis. 

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] Does parallel, attention-based fusion (TR-MISR) match or surpass the
    recurrent ConvGRU fusion (SOFI-MISRGRU) in super-resolution quality across signal-to-noise
    and blinking conditions, and at what inference-latency cost?
  % \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] How does each fusion mechanism behave when the number of input frames
  %   at inference differs from the number seen during training, and what explains the
  %   difference?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] Can SOFI-MISRGRU be restructured into a streaming architecture that
    emits one super-resolved image per incoming frame, enabling continuous real-time SR video,
    and how does its quality and per-output latency compare to the batch model?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] How does inter-frame sample motion degrade SR quality, 
  and does fine-tuning with motion augmentation recover it?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

In answering these questions, this thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Adaptation of the TR-MISR transformer fusion module to second-order SOFI
    super-resolution, including a single-channel encoder, a decoder matched to the fractional
    SOFI output size, and a CrossAttentionAggregator that separates frame-to-frame
    communication from final feature aggregation. We also correct the attention-score scaling
    of the original TR-MISR; this fix is required for the multi-head configurations used in
    our size ablation, but has no effect on the single-head (1h1d) model we ultimately deploy
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:method}).
  \item A systematic comparison of TR-MISR and SOFI-MISRGRU across various blinking and
    SNR conditions, showing that attention-based fusion holds its quality under low-SNR,
    slow-blinking acquisitions where the GRU degrades, but does \emph{not} reduce inference
    latency over the RESURF~\cite{resurf2024} baseline (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).
  \item An analysis of the frame-count generalisation of both architectures, tracing
    MISRGRU's degradation to its unnormalised aggregation and showing that TR-MISR trained on
    eight frames can be evaluated on longer sequences without retraining.
  \item A streaming SOFI-MISRGRU that emits one super-resolved output per input frame,
    reaching the reconstruction quality of batch baseline and improving with more accumulated
    frames while cutting the per-output latency to RESURF~\cite{resurf2024} baseline by 10 times.
    This is the main low-latency contribution of the thesis.
  \item A motion-robust extension of the streaming model, combining motion-augmented training with
    a Deformable Phase-space Alignment module that sustains reconstruction quality under
    inter-frame sample drift. 
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} provides background on fluorescence microscopy, SOFI
theory, and the deep-learning building blocks underlying both architectures.
Chapter~\ref{ch:method} describes the adaptations made to TR-MISR and MISRGRU
for this application, including the encoder, Transformer fusion module, decoder
variants, and the motion augmentation training strategy.

research question in turn.
