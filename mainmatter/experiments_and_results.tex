\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter evaluates the two model families introduced earlier against the central
question of whether deep-learning models can replace SOFI for fast, live-cell imaging.
We first assess the TR-MISR transformer: how reconstruction quality scales with the number
of fused frames, the effect of the training frame count, and the trade-off between
transformer size and inference latency. We then turn to MISRGRU, modifying it for video
super-resolution through a sliding-window variant and a streaming formulation that emits one
super-resolved frame per input, and we study how the accumulation window governs the
quality–latency balance for continuous imaging. Throughout, models are compared using
metrics chosen for SOFI reconstructions (Resolution Scaled Pearson correlation and Decorrelation 
analysis). Encoder, decoder, loss-function and other hyperparameter choices are validated by
ablation in Appendix~\ref{app:trnet_modifications}.

\section{Evaluating Performance of TRMISR}
\label{sec:arch_compare}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{ssec:experimental_setup}

\subsubsection{Model Training Details}
For the following TR-MISR experiments the architecture with a PixelShuffle decoder was used
with a and a lightweight fusion module (1 attention head and depth of 1 - 1h1d), 
trained on 8 and 20 input frames for 100 epochs.
Models are optimised with Adam using a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ for the encoder/decoder
and $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for the transformer, reduced on validation-loss plateau.
The combined L1+Fourier loss is used throughout; 2000 simulated samples are used with a
90/10 train/validation split. The choice of loss function and decoder were
validated by ablation; see Appendix~\ref{app:trnet_modifications}.

The test set comprises 12 SNR conditions spanning three blinking speeds: 
10/600, 10/1200 or 10/2400 corresponding to On and Off times in miliseconds
and four photon levels (100\%, 80\%, 60\%, 40\% of nominal),
with 40 samples per condition (480 test samples total).
For simplicity we often report only 4 conditions: fast and slow combined 
with highest and lowest photon levels.

Models are evaluated with the two metrics introduced in Section~\ref{ssec:metrics}:
resolution-scaled Pearson correlation (RSP) for structural fidelity and decorrelation
analysis for spatial resolution. RSP is the primary metric reported in all experiments;
decorrelation analysis is reported only for the best models of each class.

\subsection{Multi-frame Fusion}
\label{ssec:multi-frame Fusion}

Because TR-MISR's cross-attention aggregator uses a softmax over all $T$ input tokens, it
is not architecturally constrained to a fixed frame count.
A model trained on 8 frames can therefore attend to longer sequences at test time without
retraining or fine-tuning. 
Sequential GRU fusion does not share this capability as we show in the experiment.

To verify this, we trained the TRNet with modifications as mentioned 
in Section~\ref{sec:adaptation}. 
The model was trained on 8 input frames with shallow transformer size (1 head and 1 depth).
During evaluation we compared the reconstruction accuracy of using different input frames $N \in \{8, 20, 30\}$ 
and compare against the MISRGRU foundational models from RESURF~\cite{resurf2024}.

Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot_rsp_model_comparison} shows RSP results using box plots calculated using
480 evaluation samples, with classical SOFI (100-frame) shown in grey on the left.

Providing more frames consistently improves TR-MISR: the 20-frame and 30-frame variants

improvement is real rather than noise: on a matched 20-frame budget TR-MISR beats
MISRGRU by $0.013$ RSP on average and on roughly $80\%$ of the paired samples
(rank-biserial $-0.81$, paired Wilcoxon $p < 10^{-50}$;
Appendix~\ref{app:significance}).

This confirms that the attention mechanism generalises to sequences longer than
those seen during training.

conditions (09--12\_slow), TR-MISR's median RSP stays closer to the 8-frame MISRGRU
baseline, whereas MISRGRU 8f falls more sharply.

Surprisingly, the MISRGRU 20-frame model performs worse on
several slow-blinking conditions when the input is extended to 30 frames.
We attribute this to the unnormalised summation in the fusion module's GAP layer, which
causes the decoder input magnitude to grow with increasing number of input frames
beyond the range seen during training.
Section~\ref{ssec:kbuf_misrgru} analyses this effect quantitatively.


\subsubsection{Resolution estimation of TRNet outputs}
To test how the number of input frames affects the output resolution we apply
decorrelation analysis~\cite{descloux2019decorrelation} to the SR outputs of TRNet
trained on 8 input frames.
Three numbers of input were tested: 8, 20, 30 across all four blinking regimes:
fast-high SNR (FH), fast-low SNR (FL), slow-high SNR (SH), and slow-low SNR (SL).

Figure~\ref{fig:imdecor_8f} shows box plots of the estimated resolution for the
model trained on 8 frames but evaluated at 8, 20 and 30 input frames. 
All three variations are able to resolve structure well below the optical diffraction limit,
confirming genuine super-resolution across all blinking configurations, averaging 
${\approx}140-150$\,nm.

The same decorrelation analysis for the RESURF~\cite{resurf2024} models and SOFI is reproduced
in Figure~\ref{fig:imdecor_resurf}. There, the MISRGRU models resolve structural detail down
to ${\approx}130$\,nm, a sharpness that is also visible qualitatively in
Figure~\ref{fig:model_reconstruction_comparison}: closely spaced microtubules are separated
more cleanly by MISRGRU, whereas TR-MISR renders thicker filaments that score well on RSP
but blur fine structure together.



\subsection{Effect of Training Frame Count: 8f vs 20f TRNet}
\label{ssec:trnet_8f_vs_20f}

To understand whether training on more frames improves TRNet, we trained an additional
model on 20-frame sequences and compared it against the 8-frame trained model across
$N \in \{20, 30\}$ input frames at test time. Both models were trained for 100 epochs.
Loss functions are in the Appendix~\ref{app:tr-misr-val-loss}
Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot_rsp_20fvs8f} shows the RSP distributions for both models.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figures/snr_conditions_no_pos_8v20.png}
  \caption{Comparison of TR-MISR model trained on 8 frames and versus TRNet trained on 20 frames,
  both are evaluated with 20 input frames, alongside foundational MISRGRU on 20 frames.
  All models are evaluated across different SNR and blinking conditions.}
  \label{fig:boxplot_rsp_20fvs8f}
\end{figure}

Counterintuitively, the 8-frame trained model outperforms the 20-frame trained model when
both are evaluated on 20.
We belive that 8 frames is already sufficient to capture enough information produced by
fluorophore blinking: at the simulated acquisition rates and blinking speeds, in 80ms time fluorophores
that were on will have switched state to dark state least once within 8 frames, so the cross-attention
module receives a diverse sample of blink events and can learn to aggregate efficiently.
Training on 20 frames does not provide substantially more blinking statistics; it
predominantly adds redundant observations of the same fluorophore states.

% Second, with 20 training frames the attention module may become \emph{over-saturated} at
% individual spatial locations: a fluorophore that is on for many consecutive frames
% produces a cluster of similar tokens that dominates the cross-attention, potentially
% suppressing weaker but informative blink events elsewhere.
% The 8-frame model, having learned to aggregate under a tighter budget, develops attention
% weights that are more robust, which is why it generalises better when
% the sequence is extended at test time.

This finding reinforces the conclusion from Section~\ref{ssec:multi-frame Fusion}: the
attention mechanism benefits from being given more frames at inference, but the model
should be \emph{trained} on a short sequence to encourage efficient use of each frame. 
This way both training and finetuning can be speed up without sacrificing the reconstruction accuracy.


\subsection{Latency vs Transformer Size Trade-offs}
\label{ssec:latency_transformer_sizes}

To figure out the best accuracy speed tradeoff of the TR-MISR model we trained four 
different models where we varied the sizes of heads and depths used in transformer fusion module.
All four configurations converge to nearly identical validation loss. 
Even though the larger variants were trained for fewer epochs due to a 24\,h limit on DelftBlue (Appendix~\ref{app:heads_val_loss}).

Figure~\ref{fig:transformer_sizes} depicts the evaluation of different using RSP metric.
Increasing the number of parameters does not improve the metric dramatically, suggesting the
fusion module reaches capacity quickly and that a single attention head with one 
transformer layer (1h1d) is sufficient to aggregate information from multiple frames    
making it the preferred configuration given its substantially lower inference latency
(Table~\ref{tab:inference_latency}).

\section{Modifying MISRGRU for Video SR Reconstruction}

\subsection{Sliding-window MISRGRU Modification for Faster Inference}
\label{ssec:kbuf_misrgru}

Subsection~\ref{ssec:multi-frame Fusion} claimed that MISRGRU oversaturates the input into the 
decoder and quality of the output degrades with the increasing number of frames. 
In this experiment we took pretrained weights from foundational models and modified the architecture
to reuse the outputs $h_t$ from convolution gru layers. We kept the weights in a queue of size 20.

The figure~\ref{fig:kbuf_degradation} shows this empirically with RSP metric as increasing number
of frames are input into the model. The model is evaluated at 1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 100 
input frames.

This experiment showed us that MISRGRU architecture with summative GAP layer is not well 
suited for multi-input-multi-output task which prompted us to change the architecture to streaming MISRGRU
which normalizes the input in the decoder to produce consistent results.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figures/kbuf_K20_conditions_vs_frames.png}
  \caption{Degrading output quality of K-buffer MISRGRU modification, across different SNR and blinking conditions.}
  \label{fig:kbuf_degradation}
\end{figure}


The GRU hidden state $h_t$ is a cumulative summary of all frames up to time $t$,
so the fused representation $\mathbf{f} = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} h_t$ is an unnormalised
accumulation: the first frame contributes to every $h_t$, giving it $T$-fold weight
relative to the final frame.
Moreover, $h_t$ tends to grow in magnitude, making the sum grow with $T$.
A decoder trained on 20 input frames therefore receives activations of a different
scale when the number of input frames is extended changed at test time.

To verify this claim empirically, we measured the L2-norm of the tensor passed to
each model's decoder ($f$) on 20 real microscopy patches from the testing set,
changing the number of input frames $T \in \{8, 20, 30\}$.
The results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:decoder_input_norm}.
For both MISRGRU variants the decoder-input norm scales beyond the linear
expectation, confirming the saturation hypothesis. 
In contrast, TR-MISR's fusion output remains almost constant across all
three frame counts (ratios of $1.02\times$ and $1.01\times$), because the
cross-attention softmax redistributes weight across frames rather than accumulating
magnitude. Interestingly 8 frame MISRGRU at $T=8$ reaches approximately same L2 norm as the
20 frame MISRGRU at $T=20$ indicating that both models decoder learnt to input similar tensor.

    \toprule
    \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Train $T$}
      & $\|\mathbf{f}\|_2\ (T{=}8)$

TR-MISR does not suffer from this issue because its cross-attention aggregator normalises
encoded information from all $T$ frames with a softmax.
Adding more frames redistributes attention weight rather than increasing output magnitude,
so the decoder always receives a input at consistent scale regardless of
how many frames are input.


\section{Streaming MISRGRU}
\label{sec:streaming}
Streaming models are evaluated by feeding frames one at a time from the test set and measuring 
RSP between the SR output at 20 and 100 accumulated frames compared 
to the Ground Truth image for 4 different imaging conditions (photons/frame): 100(high-SNR) or 40 (low-SNR), and
and blinking conditions (on time/ off time in ms): 10/600 (fast) or 10/2400 (slow). The main benefit we want to test
in this experiment is whether the model can achive better reconstruction results with more accumulated images. 

Experiments with ablations and hyperparameter tunings are described in 
appendix~\ref{app:streaming_misrgru_ablations}. The final best performing model is evaluated in this
section with following configuration:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Loss Last N: 35 with TBPTT supervision
  \item Fourier-only loss
  \item Single 48-channel ConvGRU
  \item Transposed-convolution decoder
\end{itemize}

At 20 accumulated frames the streaming model already matches or slightly exceeds the
batch MISRGRU baseline on every condition (FH/FL/SH/SL: Streaming $0.913/0.913/0.884/0.878$
vs.\ Batch $0.911/0.901/0.875/0.858$).
Pooled over the full test set this edge is highly consistent rather than incidental: at a
matched 20-frame budget the streaming model wins on almost every paired sample
(mean gain $0.010$ RSP, rank-biserial $-0.99$, paired Wilcoxon $p < 10^{-70}$;
Appendix~\ref{app:significance}).
With all accumulated frames from the acquisition (100 frames)
RSP rises to $0.929/0.927/0.916/0.888$, a consistent gain over the 20-frame batch model
that is more pronounced in the harder slow and low-SNR conditions
(Figure~\ref{fig:lln35_vs_foundational_misrgru}).
The batch MISRGRU cannot benefit in this way, as its fixed 20-frame input window and GAP fusion mechanism
prevents it from integrating additional context and TRMISR is limited by its compute and memory. 
The largest RSP gain, from $0.875$ to $0.916$, 
occurred under slow blinking and high SNR.
This is expected: under slow blinking each emitter's on/off cycle is long relative to the
accumulation window, so 20 frames undersample the fluctuation statistics, and additional
frames capture more independent blinking events and yield a better cumulant estimate. 
(Figure~\ref{fig:Qualitative_comparison}).


\paragraph{Decorrelation analysis} estimates the ground-truth images themselves at approximately
139 nm, which sets the resolution ceiling that the model could reach if it reconstructed
the image perfectly.
Accumulating more frames improves both metrics, but to differing degrees: RSP improves
substantially (Figure~\ref{fig:lln35_vs_foundational_misrgru}), while the corresponding
resolution estimates (Figure~\ref{fig:imdecor_comparison}) improve only slightly relative
to outputs reconstructed from 20 frames.

\subsection{Finding the Optimal Accumulation Window}
\label{ssec:accumulating_frames}
Accumulating more frames improves reconstruction quality, but at the cost of temporal
resolution, since each additional frame delays the moment a fully-informed reconstruction
becomes available. To find the point at which this trade-off saturates, we fed the streaming
MISRGRU sequences of up to 500 frames and recorded the RSP of the output after
$N \in \{8, 20, 50, 75, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500\}$ accumulated frames. Because each simulated
acquisition contains only 100 frames, the sequence is looped once all 100 frames have been
ingested. The experiment was run on the four corner SNR conditions (FH, FL, SH, SL);
Figure~\ref{fig:accumulating_frames} reports the resulting RSP distributions.

Across all conditions, quality improves with first $\sim$50 frames and then plateaus.
Beyond this peak, quality no longer improves and the slow conditions even decline slightly:
SL falls from $\sim$0.90 at 75 frames to $\sim$0.85 by 200 frames and stays there, while fast
blinking dataset settles just below its peak. The fast conditions hold their plateau out to 500 frames. 
This decline in the high-frame regime should be read with caution, since past 100 frames the
looped sequence re-presents already-seen frames rather than new fluctuation statistics. 

Taken together, accumulating roughly 50--75 frames captures essentially all of the attainable
quality while keeping the temporal resolution as high as possible, making it the preferred
operating point for the streaming model. 

Notably, this saturation point coincides with the training window: the streaming model was
supervised only on the first 50 frames of each simulated acquisition, so it is hard to tell
whether the model is genuinely unable to integrate information beyond 50 accumulated frames or
whether the cumulant estimate itself has simply saturated.

\section{Motion reconstruction}
\label{sec:alignment_ablation}
The low per-frame latency of the streaming MISRGRU makes it a natural
candidate for imaging samples that move during acquisition, where the static-scene
assumption underlying SOFI breaks down. We therefore evaluated how well the streaming
models reconstruct moving microtubule structures.

To test whether explicit inter-frame alignment helps, we trained a streaming variant
incorporating the Deformable Phase-space Alignment module (DPA) inspired by
\cite{qiao2025dpatisr}; implementation details are given in subsection~\ref{sssec:dpa}.
Both motion variants were initialised from the converged fourier baseline streaming MISRGRU weights
(Figure~\ref{fig:largergru_vs_fourierbaseline}) and finetuned on motion-augmented data.
The plain motion-finetuned model (\texttt{Finetune-motion}) was trained for 100 epochs;
because the DPA variant converged faster and reached higher reconstruction quality, we
trained it with lln35 weights (Figure~\ref{fig:lln10vslln35}) for 169 epochs
(\texttt{DPA}). 
The remaining three rows of Table~\ref{tab:rsp_combined} are 
unmodified, static-trained baselines included as
references: the larger streaming GRU (\texttt{LLN35-larger-GRU}), the batch
\texttt{MISR-GRU}, and the batch \texttt{TR-MISR}.

We evaluated reconstruction accuracy under three acquisition speeds (static,
$\approx 2.0\,\mu$m/s, and $\approx 5.0\,\mu$m/s) across the four corner SNR conditions
(FH, FL, SH, SL).

Table~\ref{tab:rsp_combined} reports the RSP at 20 accumulated frames. The motion-augmented
DPA streaming variant retains strong reconstruction quality on both static and moving samples, whereas the
static-trained baselines collapse. At $2.0\,\mu$m/s and fast blinking the \texttt{DPA}
model on moving data matches its own static performance and even exceeds the static
\texttt{TR-MISR} baseline ($93.3 \pm 0.7$ vs.\ $90.7 \pm 0.8$), while the static-trained
\texttt{TR-MISR} and \texttt{MISR-GRU} fall below $73$ at the same speed. As expected,
absolute quality is lower in the slow-blinking conditions (SH, SL) for every model, since
fewer fluorophores are simultaneously ON and each accumulated frame carries less
information about the underlying microtubule structure.

The visual comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:motion_visual_comparison} mirrors these numbers.
The static-trained \texttt{TR-MISR} and batch \texttt{MISR-GRU} cannot reconstruct the
moving sample: they accumulate previously seen blinks without resolving the underlying
structure, so the output resembles an averaged blur. Both motion-finetuned streaming models
recover fine detail the \texttt{DPA} variant additionally shows slightly less
ghosting, visible in the top part of the image.

\subsection{Streaming Models Inference Latency}
\label{ssec:streaming_latency}

The architectural distinction between batch and streaming inference has a direct consequence
on throughput: the batch MISRGRU consumes $N$ frames and outputs a single SR
image, whereas the streaming variant emits one SR output for every input frame.
Models were benchmarked on an NVIDIA RTX A5000 using $512\times512$ input frames,
5 warmup passes, and 20 timed passes.

All three streaming variants produce reconstructed outputs far more efficiently per frame:
they match the accuracy of the batch MISRGRU by the 20th frame and continue to improve as
more frames accumulate, while emitting one SR image per input frame rather than a single
output after the full sequence. The base streaming MISRGRU produces each SR frame in only
$11.29$\,ms and the DPA variant in $17.46$\,ms. Processing the full 20-frame sequence is
nonetheless slower than the batch model ($226$\,ms and $349$\,ms versus $170$\,ms), because
the decoder is invoked once per frame rather than a single time at the end.

Both variants stay well under the $20$\,ms budget required for real-time reconstruction at
$50$\,fps, making the streaming MISRGRU an efficient choice for reconstructing an entire
acquisition continuously, frame by frame, without the need to restart the model. Since the
DPA aligns the frames together we achieve temporal-resolution equal to camera acquisition rate.

Decorrelation analysis results closely match the results of staticaly trained streaming MISRGRU
model. Both models achieved better resolution at 100 accumulated frames 
(Figure~\ref{fig:imdecor_comparison_longer_dpa}).
