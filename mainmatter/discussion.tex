\chapter{Discussion}                                                                   
\label{ch:discussion}                                                                  
                 
Having real-time feedback of the reconstruction matters a lot for live-cell imaging because
it allows biologists to observe the live sample as it interacts with the world and intervene
if something goes wrong. Ideally, a smart microscope could act on this feedback on its own,
adjusting the acquisition without an operator in the loop.
In a post-processing workflow the
low-resolution frames are collected and reconstructed only afterwards, so failures such as
focus drift, photobleaching, an incorrect exposure, or the cell moving out of the field of
view may go unnoticed until the session is over and the data is analysed. A streaming
reconstruction overcomes these problems while the experiment is still running, allowing the
operator to refocus, reposition the sample, adjust the imaging parameters before the data
quality degrades, or abort an unproductive acquisition early. This whole thesis aimed towards 
how could such streaming reconstruction be implemented.

\section{Does parallel attention improve upon recurrent fusion?}
                                                                                        
The main motivation of this work was to test whether replacing the sequential       
ConvGRU fusion in MISRGRU~\cite{resurf2024} with a Transformer's parallel
self-attention would yield a meaningful improvement in either reconstruction quality 
or inference speed:
In aggregate resolution-scaled Pearson correlation, TRMISR with a shallow transformer (1h1d) is competitive with
but does not consistently outperform the 20-frame MISRGRU foundational model on fast 
blinking and high SNR conditions. However the attention advantage starts to show when models 
are evaluated on worse conditions where TRMISR achieved consistently higher RSP. This finding is consistent
with our hypothesis that transformer fusion is more robust to noisy conditions.
But on raw latency it is slower (262\,ms vs.\ 170\,ms at $N{=}20$,
Table~\ref{tab:inference_latency}).                                                    
Therefore, the attention mechanism does not give a clear answer wether to use it over convolutional
recurrence as it depends on acquisition conditions such as blinking and signal-to-noise ratio and 
compute constraints.

Practical advantage of TRMISR is that the     
model trained on 8 frames can be infered on 20, 30 or 40 frames (if the memory allows it) 
at test time with no architectural change, whereas extending MISRGRU beyond its training length 
causes progressive reconstruction degradation. TRMISR's the cross-attention softmax normalises over all input tokens, so the magnitude of the
fused representation remains stable regardless of how many frames are presented at inference      
(Table~\ref{tab:decoder_input_norm}).
Batch MISRGRU's summative GAP layer accumulates and oversaturates which degrades the output of the network 
removing the ability of feeding more input frames at inference time.
                           
This property is valuable in practice as it means that the model can be trained on shorter training sequences 
reduce compute cost and convergence time, while the researcher still has the option of feeding more frames   
for improved quality at inference. 
                                                                                        
An additional finding reinforced this:                                         
Counterintuitively, a TRNet trained on 8 frames outperforms one trained on 20 frames
when both are evaluated on 20- or 30-frame inputs                                      
(Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot_rsp_20fvs8f}).
We believe this happened because the training under the   
8-frame constraint forces the attention module to assign meaningful and more discriminative    
weights to every available frame.                                                         
                                                                                        
Ablating the Transformer fusion across four configurations (1h1d, 2h2d, 4h3d, 8h6d)    
shows that RSP does not improve meaningfully beyond 1h1d (Figure~\ref{fig:transformer_sizes}), 
while latency grows steeply: going from 1h1d to 4h3d triples the parameter count 
but increases $N{=}8$ latency by 2.4$\times$. 
Both enlarging the Transformer fusion module and widening the encoder do improve
reconstruction quality, but only at a steep latency cost: the gains are statistically
significant yet small in mean RSP, while latency grows several-fold.                          
% Future improvements should focus on the encoder and decoder blocks (e.g.\ deeper residual
% blocks, deformable convolutions) 
% rather than increasing the size of the transformers.                      

A natural architectural extension is a streaming TRMISR. This thesis pairs a Streaming MISRGRU
with a batch Transformer, but never a streaming Transformer, leaving one cell of the design
space unexplored. Since attention degraded more gracefully than recurrence under the harder
blinking and low-SNR conditions, an attention variant with a key-value cache that
emits one output per frame could combine the reconstruction robustness of TRMISR with the
throughput of the streaming GRU. 

\section{Towards Video Super-Resolution - Streaming MISRGRU}                                
With TRMISR's substantially slower inference speed,
we saw clearer path to try optimize the MISRGRU for real-time video super-resolution 
during microscope acquisitions.
We explored reusing weights from foundational MISRGRU with sliding-window approach, quickly realizing the 
GAP bottleneck as well as the hidden layer should already encode most of the features.

The batch model produces one SR output per $N$ acquired frames; the streaming model
produces one output per frame, amortising the processing cost over the entire $N$ Frame 
acquisition.
At $N{=}20$ frames on a 512$\times$512 field of view, streaming MISRGRU takes 11.29\,ms using PyTorch
per SR output compared to 170.29\,ms for the batch model a $15\times$ improvement in
per-output latency.
The latency results reported here are obtained with PyTorch inference. Compiling the models
with TensorRT and applying FP16 or INT8 quantisation could potentially half the inference times.


Streaming MISRGRU RSP converges to the batch-model value by the 20th accumulated frame
(Figure~\ref{fig:accumulating_frames}), and continues to improve beyond that     
point as the GRU accumulates more context. 

We found that the reconstruction quality saturates at around 50 accumulated frames, which sets
the practical operating point for the streaming model. At a 100\,fps acquisition rate this
corresponds to a temporal resolution of 0.5\,s. Because the model emits one super-resolved
output for every frame it receives, the reconstruction also captures motion implicitly: a
structure that moves over the accumulation window appears blurred in the output, signalling
the presence and direction of motion to the operator.

\section{Motion reconstruction robustness}                                                  
Real-time imaging of moving structures was achieved with
motion-augmented training.
We compared finetuning the baseline model against adding a
Deformable Phase-space Alignment (DPA) module.
The final DPA model proved effectively motion-invariant: at fast/high-SNR its RSP held near
0.93 from static through $5\,\mu$m/s, while the static-trained streaming, batch MISRGRU and
TR-MISR baselines collapsed (below 0.73 at $2\,\mu$m/s and 0.55 at $5\,\mu$m/s) because they
fuse spatially misaligned frames into a blur. Plain finetuning also handled motion but
trailed DPA by several RSP points and, on visual inspection, left more blur and ghosting
artefacts while converging more slowly.

Importantly, the motion-finetuned DPA model outperforms the statically trained Streaming MISRGRU
 on both static and motion augmented evaluation dataset. TRMISR still achieves higher reconstruction accuracy
 on slow blinking and low signal-to-noise ratio images; this
 crossover between DPA and TR-MISR is significant when compared for each blinking and SNR condition separately
 (Appendix~\ref{app:significance}).

Degradation concentrated in the slow conditions where temporal averaging is most valuable.
This trade-off is expected: a model trained to handle inter-frame displacement     
cannot simultaneously exploit the long-range temporal correlation that static,    
slow-blinking acquisitions provide.                                                    
For deployment, the static streaming model is prefered when the sample is
known to be stationary or the drift is below the model's tolerance; the                
motion-finetuned or DPA variant should be selected when the sample is expected to exhibits visible  
drift relative to the field of view.

\section{Reflection in the broader DSAIT context}

This section places the work in the wider Data Science and Artificial Intelligence
Technology (DSAIT) field and discusses what it means for the people who would use it.

\subsection{Relation to the broader field}
At a higher level, this thesis fuses a temporal sequence of observations into a single
high-quality output under a tight latency budget, a problem that appears well beyond
fluorescence microscopy. The central question, whether parallel attention should replace
recurrent processing, repeats the shift the field went through when Transformers replaced
recurrent networks in language and vision. Here that shift did not carry over cleanly as recurrent
architecture achieved lower latency and better results on high signal-to-noise data.

The work also reuses an architecture built for one domain in a very different one, a common
pattern in DSAIT. This required real adaptation and domain knowledge rather than drop-in
reuse, which shows that such transfer is rarely free. Finally, the work treats inference
latency and deployability as primary goals, placing it in the area of efficient machine
learning, where the aim is comparable quality in real time on ordinary hardware rather than a
higher accuracy number. This matters as AI moves from offline benchmarks into live systems.

\subsection{Implications, applicability, and stakeholders}
The direct users, the biologists operating the instrument, benefit most: real-time feedback
turns a passive recording into an interactive process and lets them correct or abandon a
measurement before it is wasted. But a tool that is easy to run and gives reasonable results
is also easy to over-trust, and that risk grows as it reaches users less able to judge when
it fails. 

The main risk of a generative
model is hallucination: it can produce plausible structure that the measurement does not
support, and it does so most in the difficult conditions where the output is hardest to
check. If such an artefact is mistaken for real signal, it can corrupt a scientific
conclusion or a clinical decision. Responsible use therefore relies on the safeguards
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: report uncertainty with the output, be clear
about the limits of the validation, and treat anything visible only in the model output as
something to confirm independently. The tool should be presented as a fast approximation of a
trusted method, not as a replacement for verification.

There is also an environmental cost. Training these models consumes energy, so a fair view of
efficiency should weigh the one-time training cost against the savings over the model's
deployed lifetime; for a model trained once and run many times, the trade is favourable.
Reproducibility is the related obligation: because the results depend on the training data,
that data and its assumptions must be documented and shared for others to trust and extend
the work.

\section{Limitations and Future Directions}
\label{sec:limitations}

First, all quantitative results are obtained on a synthetic microtubule dataset
generated with the simulation pipeline from~\cite{resurf2024}. Transfer to real
experimental data is not addressed in this thesis. The most immediate direction for
future work is to establish whether the models can reconstruct live-cell acquisitions.

A one promising direction for adapting synthetically trained models to a specific
microscope is to fine-tune the network on a sequence of blank background frames, 
that capture only detector noise and autofluorescence. This way the model could 
quickly adapt to noise levels \cite{noise2noise2018}, and extract the signal easier. 

Once the models prove capable of reconstructing live-cell data, whether through
fine-tuning or out of the box, a natural next step is to pursue general-purpose
super-resolution. Most current methods~\cite{qiao2025dpatisr,resurf2024} must be fine-tuned
for the specific sample being imaged, or different PSF. We believe that deep-learning models could instead
operate like SOFI, generalising across acquisitions without being retrained for a particular
point-spread function or sample structure.


A further important direction is to equip the models with uncertainty estimates~\cite{qiao2025dpatisr}.
The most serious risk of replacing SOFI with a trained model is hallucination: we ask the network
to recover structure that SOFI itself does not resolve, yet in doing so the model may invent
connections between microtubules that are absent from the ground truth. Per-pixel uncertainty maps
could serve as a built-in sanity check, quantifying how confident the model is about each part of
the reconstruction. Such confidence information is essential before any structure visible only in
the network output is treated as real.

Our motion augmentation also has a structural limitation: it applies a single global
displacement to the entire field of view, translating the whole structure rather than its
individual parts. Real biological motion is rarely rigid. A model trained only on globally translated
sequences may therefore generalise poorly to such local, non-rigid deformation. Although, the model 
uses the local deformable convolution to limit this dependancy on global movement,
 but it was not tested in practice. 
Future research can focus on extending the augmentation to per-region or non-rigid displacement fields, 
and pairing it with a DPA module that estimates spatially varying alignment.

Finally, training against higher-order SOFI targets could push the spatial resolution further.
This would require simulating additional data and generating the corresponding higher-order
SOFI targets, but the decoder layers can be readily adapted to estimate higher-order cumulants.
